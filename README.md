# Physics: a connected map of knowledge

This repository is a long-term project to organize physics into a useful, navigable knowledge system. The aim is not to replace textbooks or claim one final taxonomy. It is to make the field easier to learn, inspect, compare, and extend.

## Guiding advice

**Organize physics as a network, not just a list of subfields.** A table of contents is useful for navigation, but many important ideas cross traditional boundaries: symmetry connects mechanics, quantum theory, and particle physics; conservation laws connect theory and experiment; statistical reasoning links microscopic dynamics to thermodynamics and materials. Record both each topic’s place in the map and its connections to other topics.

## A practical structure for each topic

A topic page should answer, where relevant:

1. **What is the central question or phenomenon?**
2. **What are the key quantities, concepts, and equations?** Define symbols and units.
3. **What assumptions and regime of validity does the model use?**
4. **How does it connect to other ideas?** Include prerequisites, shared principles, limits, and useful analogies.
5. **What evidence supports it?** Link to experiments, observations, or reliable references.
6. **What remains open or uncertain?** Separate established results from active research and speculation.

Keep derivations, examples, historical context, and references close to the concept they explain. Link across pages rather than copying the same explanation into many places.

## Initial top-level map

- **Mathematical and experimental foundations** — measurement, uncertainty, units, estimation, modeling, data, instruments, and computation.
- **Core principles** — states, dynamics, interactions, symmetry, invariance, conservation, causality, and scale.
- **Classical physics** — mechanics, fields, electromagnetism, waves, fluids, optics, and classical thermodynamics.
- **Quantum physics** — quantum mechanics, quantum information, quantum statistical mechanics, and quantum field theory.
- **Relativity and gravitation** — special relativity, general relativity, gravitational waves, and tests of gravity.
- **Statistical and many-body physics** — statistical mechanics, condensed matter, materials, phase transitions, and complex systems.
- **Particles and nuclei** — particle physics, nuclear physics, and the structure of matter.
- **Plasmas and astrophysical systems** — plasma physics, stars, compact objects, galaxies, and high-energy astrophysics.
- **Cosmology** — the large-scale universe, its history, composition, and open questions.
- **Cross-cutting tools and methods** — computation, numerical modeling, effective theories, precision measurement, and uncertainty analysis.

These are entry points, not sealed boxes. Topics can belong to several branches, and the map should show that.

## How to grow the project

1. Start with a small set of well-sourced overview pages and a clear glossary.
2. Add prerequisite and connection links as concepts are documented.
3. Label claims by status: established, model-dependent, unresolved, or speculative.
4. Record sources with enough detail for another reader to find and check them.
5. Review the map periodically for missing links, duplicated explanations, unclear assumptions, and areas that need expert review.

## Working principles

- Prefer precise, plain explanations over unexplained jargon.
- State assumptions, approximations, units, and validity limits.
- Distinguish evidence from interpretation.
- Use primary sources for important or contested claims, alongside trustworthy textbooks and reviews.
- Keep competing models and legitimate disagreements visible.
- Treat this map as a revisable guide; changes should improve accuracy and usefulness, not enforce a single philosophical interpretation of physics.
